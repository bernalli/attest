attest

Own what you buy. The seller signs a receipt, you hold the file, anyone can verify it offline — even after the store is gone.

attest is the durable, portable, user-held layer of ownership for digital content. The seller signs a receipt once at checkout; the buyer keeps the file; anyone can verify it offline afterwards — issuer, license grant, buyer binding — with no surviving service of any kind. It works today wherever files are sold DRM-free, and it is built so a regulator can require it of closed platforms: the purchase confirmation the law already mandates, in a form a machine can verify and you can carry away.

Prove a receipt is yours (salt disclosure)

A receipt seals a commitment to the buyer’s identifier. Disclosing the salt lets a verifier recompute it — a deliberate, replayable proof: whoever you show it to can replay it, so disclose only to parties you would show the receipt to anyway (spec §8.1). This page stores nothing.

Who this is for

A seller who ships DRM-free files can start signing this afternoon, without asking anyone's permission: every buyer walks away with content plus proof, both in their hands. That is the first track — the proof still verifies when the shop is gone — and it needs nobody's approval to work.

The second track is the closed platforms, the ones that will never sign voluntarily. In the EU they are already required to confirm every purchase on a durable medium — Article 8(7) of the Consumer Rights Directive (2011/83/EU) — which is why confirmation emails exist. attest is that same confirmation in a format a machine can verify and a buyer can take away, so a regulator has a usable format to point at instead of a new obligation to invent.

Being honest about where this stands: no store issues attest receipts yet, and no regulator mandates them. The standard, two independent implementations and the conformance suite are done and free to use. What is missing is the first seller who signs.

How it works

  1. The store signs. At checkout, the seller signs a receipt — title, terms, artifact hashes, survivability promises — with its published signing keys.
  2. You hold it. The buyer keeps the .attest bundle anywhere: disk, cloud, USB. No account has to stay alive.
  3. Anyone verifies, offline. Any verifier — this page, the CLI, a marketplace — checks the signature against the issuer’s key material. The store can disappear; the math doesn’t.

What attest is not

  • Not the content. A receipt is evidence of a license grant — it never hosts, indexes, or unlocks the work itself.
  • Not a DRM tool. It never strips or bypasses DRM, and a receipt for a DRM-bound purchase says so honestly.
  • Not a blockchain. A signed receipt needs no consensus, token, or wallet. (v0.2 ships a strictly optional transparency and anchoring layer, and transfers record a chain of title — neither needs one.)
  • Not a resale market. v0.1 defines no transfer protocol at all; v0.2 §17 adds transfer only where the issuer mediates it, and never a general right to resell.
  • Not a shield against a live seller. "Forever" holds against the store disappearing, not against a live store declaring one of its own signing keys compromised: that declaration still destroys the receipts signed with that key that were never logged and anchored before it.
